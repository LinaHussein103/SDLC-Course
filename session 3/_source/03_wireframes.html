How to use this pack

Teaching sequence

A

Study the current design (pages 3–8). Seven screens of a library app that technically meets the requirements. Walk through them in order as Omar, the student below.

B

Analyse and redesign in pairs (pages 9–11). List problems, explain their impact, prioritise, sketch a better flow. 15–20 minutes.

C

Instructor reveal (page 12 onward). A reference redesign, then the answer key (Part D). Do not show these pages before the activity.

Instructor tip: print or share pages 1–11 for students. Keep pages 12–23 for projection after the activity.

Instructions

1

Examine the flawed wireframes (A1–A7).

2

Identify at least five usability problems.

3

Explain why each problem affects the user.

4

Prioritise the most serious problems.

5

Sketch a redesigned homepage, catalogue or reservation flow.

6

Explain the reasoning behind your decisions — link them to user goals or requirements in Handout 02.

Remember: a problem is not “I don’t like the colour”. Describe what the user is trying to do, what goes wrong, and what it costs them (time, errors, confusion, exclusion).

Discussion questions

What is the user’s primary goal?
Which steps are unnecessary?
Where should authentication happen?
How should the interface display availability?
How should unavailable books be handled?
How should physical collection be communicated?
How can the student monitor their borrowing period?
How should the system communicate errors?
What should change on mobile?
How can the librarian complete routine actions faster?

The improved design — reference solution

Mobile-first student app · desktop librarian dashboard

The redesign keeps exactly the same capabilities as Part A — search, details, reservation, waitlist, notifications, loans, fines and librarian processing. What changes is order, priority, wording and feedback. It is a proposed reference, not the only correct answer.

1 · Goal first

The first screen answers “do you have this book?” — search is the hero, sign-in is optional.

2 · Honest status

Availability counts real copies. Reservation states separate “Preparing” from “Ready for pickup”.

3 · Physical world visible

Pickup location, hours, 48-hour window and ID requirement appear wherever a decision is made.

4 · One primary action

Each screen has one clear, labelled main button. Destructive actions ask for confirmation.

Screens in this part: C1 Explore · C2 Search results (+ empty state) · C3 Book details (available / unavailable) · C4 Contextual sign-in · C5 Confirmation · C6 My Books (+ cancel dialog) · C7 Notifications (+ loading & error states) · C8 Librarian dashboard · Visual system.

Bad UX vs. missing functionality
The Part A design can do everything the SRS asks — browse, search, reserve, waitlist (“Hold”), cancel, notifications (“Messages”), fines, and every librarian action (via the “Action” menu). Most problems are therefore UX problems: the capability exists but is slow, hidden or misleading. Three items are different: rows 1, 2 and 18 directly contradict requirements (FR-07, FR-05, FR-08) — these are requirements misunderstandings, not matters of taste.

This is a proposed reference, not the answer
Several choices are trade-offs: e.g. hiding bibliographic detail helps students but may slow researchers; a 4-tab bar is simple but leaves no room for future features. Before building, a real team would validate it with usability testing:
  • 5–8 students: find a book, reserve it, find what to collect and what is owed.
  • 2–3 librarians: process a morning queue and a busy pickup hour.
  • Measure task success, time on task, errors and confidence; then iterate.
Designing software means understanding the users, their goals, the requirements and the consequences of every design decision.
